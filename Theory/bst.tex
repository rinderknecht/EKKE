\begin{frame}{Binary Search Trees}
  \label{frame:balanced}

  Searching in a binary tree can be costly because, in the worst case,
  the whole tree must be traversed.

  \bigskip

  To improve upon the worst case, two scenarios are desirable:
  \begin{enumerate}

    \item the binary tree should be as \textbf{balanced} as possible,
      that is, the nodes should lie as close as possible to the root
      (for a given notion of distance), and

    \item for a given tree, \textbf{only the left or the right subtree
      should be visited}, a decision taken solely by comparing the
      root and the sought key.

  \end{enumerate}

  The simplest solution consists in satisfying the second goal, and
  see later how to also reach the first.

\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------
%
\begin{frame}{Binary Search Trees}

  A \textbf{binary search tree} is a binary tree where the root (if
  any) is greater than or equal to the nodes in the left subtree, and
  lower than the nodes in the right subtree.

  \bigskip

  In the context of search trees, the nodes are called \textbf{keys},
  and they need to be \textbf{totally ordered} (that is, the fact that
  any two keys must be comparable).

  \bigskip

  A consequence is that the inorder traversal of a binary search tree
  is the list of its keys sorted in non\hyp{}decreasing order.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Searching}

  Let consider checking the \textbf{membership} of a key~\(k\) in a
  binary search tree.
  \begin{itemize}

    \item Any key is absent from the empty tree.

    \item If the tree is not empty, it has the shape $\fun{Int}(x,
      t_1, t_2)$. We compare~\(k\) with~\(x\): if equal, we found it;
      if lower, we resume the search in~\(t_1\), else in~\(t_2\).

  \end{itemize}

  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\quad}l@{}}
      \fun{mem}(k, \fun{Ext}()) & \rightarrow & \fun{False}();\\
      \fun{mem}(k, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{mem}(k, t_1), & \text{if} \; k \prec x; \\
      \fun{mem}(k, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{mem}(k, t_2), & \text{if} \; x \prec k; \\
      \fun{mem}(k, \fun{Int}(k, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{True}().
    \end{array}
  \end{equation*}

  \smallskip

  Note how the last rule features a \textbf{non\hyp{}linear
    pattern}. Otherwise, we would have to use the condition $x = k$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Leaf insertion}

  Since all unsuccessful searches end at an external node, it is
  tempting to start the insertion of a unique key by an unsuccessful
  search and then grow a leaf with the new key at the external node we
  reached.

  \bigskip

  If the key is already present in the tree, we decide to leave the
  tree unchanged.

  \bigskip

  Let us modify the membership function like so:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\quad}l@{}}
      \fun{add\_leaf}(k, \fun{Ext}()) & \rightarrow & \fun{Int}(k, \fun{Ext}(), \fun{Ext}());\\
      \fun{add\_leaf}(k, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{Int}(x, \fun{add\_leaf}(k, t_1), t_2), & \text{if} \; k \prec x; \\
      \fun{add\_leaf}(k, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{Int}(x, t_1, \fun{add\_leaf}(k, t_2)), & \text{if} \; x \prec k; \\
      \fun{add\_leaf}(k, \fun{Int}(k, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{Int}(k, t_1, t_2).
    \end{array}
  \end{equation*}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Root insertion}

  If recently inserted keys are looked up, the cost is relatively high
  because these keys are leaves or close to a leaf.

  \bigskip

  In this scenario, instead of inserting a key as a leaf, it is better
  to insert it as a root.

  \bigskip

  The idea is to perform a leaf insertion and, on the way back to the
  root (that is to say, after the recursive calls are evaluated, one
  after the other), we perform rotations to bring the inserted node up
  to the root.

  \bigskip

  More precisely, if the node was inserted in a left subtree, then a
  \textbf{right rotation} brings it one level up, otherwise a
  \textbf{left rotation} has the same effect.

  \bigskip

  The composition of these rotations brings the leaf to the root.

\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------
%
\begin{frame}{Right rotation}

  The right rotation can be depicted as
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[bb=71 679 173 721]{rotr}
  \end{center}

  We can see that \(x\)~goes `up' and \(y\)~goes `down' one level
  (this is a local transformation).

  \bigskip

  Let us define `\fun{rotr}' (right rotation) like so:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\quad}l@{}}
      \fun{rotr}(\fun{Ext}()) & \rightarrow & \fun{Ext}();\\
      \fun{rotr}(\fun{Int}(y, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2), t_3)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{Int}(x, t_1, \fun{Int}(y, t_2, t_3)).
    \end{array}
  \end{equation*}

\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------------
%
\begin{frame}{Left rotation}

  The left rotation can be depicted as
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[bb=71 679 173 721]{rotl}
  \end{center}

  We can see that \(x\)~goes `down' and \(y\)~goes `up' one level
  (this is a local transformation).

  \bigskip

  Let us defined `\fun{rotl}' (left rotation) like so:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\quad}l@{}}
      \fun{rotl}(\fun{Ext}()) & \rightarrow & \fun{Ext}();\\
      \fun{rotl}(\fun{Int}(x, t_1, \fun{Int}(y, t_2, t_3))) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{Int}(y, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2), t_3).
    \end{array}
  \end{equation*}

  Left and right rotations are the \textbf{inverse} of each other:
  \begin{equation*}
    \forall t. \fun{rotl} (\fun{rotr} \; t) \equiv
    \fun{rotr} (\fun{rotl} \; t) \equiv t.
    \end{equation*}

\end{frame}

  and then a series of rotations, left and right, bring it to the
  root:

  \bigskip
  \includegraphics[bb=71 637 412 721]{insr_ex}

  The transitive closure \;\((\twoheadrightarrow)\) captures the

  \bigskip

  Note also that the inorder traversal of all those trees remains
  invariant and a sorted list.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Root insertion}

  It is now a simple matter to modify the definition of
  \fun{add\_leaf} so it becomes root insertion \fun{add\_root} as follows:
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\quad}l@{}}
      \fun{add\_root}(k, \fun{Ext}()) & \rightarrow & \fun{Int}(k, \fun{Ext}(), \fun{Ext}());\\
      \fun{add\_root}(k, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \textcolor{blue}{\fun{rotr}(}\fun{Int}(x, \fun{add\_root}(k, t_1), t_2)\textcolor{blue}{)},
      & \text{if} \; k \prec x; \\
      \fun{add\_root}(k, \fun{Int}(x, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \textcolor{blue}{\fun{rotl}(}\fun{Int}(x, t_1, \fun{add\_root}(k, t_2))\textcolor{blue}{)},
      & \text{if} \; x \prec k; \\
      \fun{add\_root}(k, \fun{Int}(k, t_1, t_2)) & \rightarrow
      & \fun{Int}(k, t_1, t_2).
    \end{array}
  \end{equation*}

  We simply added a right rotation just after performing a left
  insertion, and a left rotation after a right insertion.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Balancing a binary search tree}

  Whether we insert a root or a leaf to a binary search tree, we may
  still obtain \textbf{degenerate trees}, that is, trees isomorphic to
  lists (each internal node has exactly one internal child, except the
  last).

  \bigskip

  You may recall that we mentioned page~\pageref{frame:balanced} the
  usefulness of balancing a search tree, so that these extreme cases
  do not happen.

  \bigskip

  There exist different criteria to balance a tree, the two most
  common relying on
  \begin{enumerate}

    \item the \textbf{distance} of a node to the root (the path
      length),

    \item the \textbf{weight} of a node (the number of nodes in its
      subtrees).

  \end{enumerate}

  Unfortunately, discussing and implementing these strategies would
  lead us too far in this lecture, but you can refer to the
  literature.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sorting with Binary Search Trees}

  We already noticed how insertions leave the inorder traversal of a
  binary search tree unchanged.

  \bigskip

  Also the inorder traversal of a binary search tree is the list of
  its keys sorted in non\hyp{}decreasing order.

  \bigskip

  Combining these two facts (which can be made formal claims and
  proved), we deduce that we can use binary search trees to sort a
  list of keys chosen from a totally ordered set.

  \bigskip

  We start by inserting each key in an empty binary search tree.

  \bigskip

  Next, we collate the inorder traversal of the final tree:
  \begin{equation*}
    \ident{sort}\; l \rightarrow \ident{inorder} (\ident{fold}(\ident{add\_leaf}, l, \fun{Ext}())).
  \end{equation*}

\end{frame}
