The \textbf{border} of a non\hyp{}empty word~\(y\) is a proper prefix
of~\(y\) which is also a suffix.

\bigskip

To understand the case of the \textbf{empty border}, consider
\(\Border{}{\word{abac}} = \varepsilon\), simply because we have
\(\word{abac} =
\underline{\varepsilon}\word{abac}\underline{\varepsilon}\).

\bigskip

For example, the word `\word{abacaba}' has three borders:
\(\varepsilon\), `\word{a}'~and~`\word{aba}'. The last one is the
\textbf{maximum border} and we write
\(\Border{}{\underline{\word{aba}}\word{c}\underline{\word{aba}}} =
\word{aba}\).

\bigskip

Note that \textbf{borders can overlap}; for example,
\(\Border{}{\underline{\word{aaa}}\word{a}} =
\Border{}{\word{a}\underline{\word{aaa}}} = \word{aaa}\).

\bigskip

The border above must be a proper prefix, so it cannot equal
`\word{aaaa}'.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Morris-Pratt algorithm}

As often when working at the level of specifications, we seek an
inductive definition for the function at hand, in this instance, the
maximum border of a word.

\bigskip

We then have the choice of finding either \(\Border{}{ay}\) or
\(\Border{}{ya}\), with \(y \neq \varepsilon\), assuming that we know
\(\Border{}{y}\).

\bigskip

Since we are interested in knowing the maximum borders of all the
\emph{prefixes} of a given pattern, the \(ya\) is more suitable (\(y
\pref ya\)).

\bigskip

The idea is to recursively consider \(\Border{}{y} \cdot a\):

\begin{itemize}

  \item if it is a prefix of~\(y\), then \(\Border{}{ya} =
    \Border{}{y} \cdot a\);

  \item otherwise, we consider the border of \(\Border{}{y} \cdot a\).

\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

      && \text{if \(\Border{}{y} \cdot a \prefeq y\)};\\
      & \Border{}{\Border{}{y} \cdot a},
      && \text{otherwise.}
    \end{aligned}
  \right.
\end{equation*}

Unfolding the equations yields:
\begin{align*}
   \Border{}{ya}
&= \Border{}{\Border{}{y} \cdot a},
& \Border{}{y} \cdot a &\nprefeq y;\\
   \Border{}{\Border{}{y} \cdot a}
&= \Border{}{\Border{2}{y} \cdot a},
&  \Border{2}{y} \cdot a &\nprefeq \Border{}{y};\\
&\;\;\smash{\vdots} & &\;\;\smash{\vdots}\\
   \smash{\Border{}{\Border{p-2}{y} \cdot a}}
&= \smash{\Border{}{\Border{p-1}{y} \cdot a}},
& \smash{\Border{p-1}{y} \cdot a} &\nprefeq \smash{\Border{p-2}{y}};
\end{align*}
and \(\varepsilon \not\in \{y,\Border{}{y}, \dots,
\Border{p-2}{y}\}\), so \(\Border{}{ya} =
\smash{\Border{}{\Border{p-1}{y} \cdot a}}\).

\Border{}{a} = \varepsilon\), else the unfolding resumes until we find
the smallest \(q > p\) such that \(\Border{}{\Border{q-1}{y} \cdot a}
= \Border{}{\Border{q}{y} \cdot a}\) with \(\Border{q}{y} \cdot a
\prefeq \Border{q-1}{y}\).

\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}

= \len{\Border{q+1}{y}},
\end{equation*}

hypothesis because \(\Border{}{y} \pref y \prefeq x\). This proves
\(\pred{P}{q+1}\) and the induction principle entails that
\(\pred{P}{n}\) holds for all \(n \geqslant 0\).

The problem here is inefficiency:
\begin{enumerate}

  \item successive calls with the same arguments trigger again the
    same computations,

  \item the recursive calls are costly.

\end{enumerate}

\bigskip

It is much preferable to have \(\MPfailureName\) as an array than as a
function:
\begin{enumerate}

  \item the values of \(\MPfailureName\) are computed only once each

  \item and are accessed in constant time instead of relying on
    function calls.

\end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Morris-Pratt algorithm}

To find out the best configuration of a pattern~\(p\) of length~\(m\)
that yields the minimum number of letter comparisons, let us assume
that the inner loop in the definition of \proc{Next} is always
executed exactly once:
\begin{align*}
  \MPfailure{x}{}{m} &= 1 + \MPfailure{x}{}{m-1} &
  x[\MPfailure{x}{}{m-1}] &= x[m-1]\\
  \MPfailure{x}{}{m-1} &= 1 + \MPfailure{x}{}{m-2} &
  x[\MPfailure{x}{}{m-2}] &= x[m-2]\\
                     & \vdots & &\vdots \\
  \MPfailure{x}{}{2} &= 1 + \MPfailure{x}{}{1} &
  x[\MPfailure{x}{}{1}] &= x[1]\\
  \MPfailure{x}{}{1} &= 1 + \MPfailure{x}{}{0}\\
  \MPfailure{x}{}{0} &= -1
\end{align*}
So \(\MPfailure{x}{}{j} = j - 1\) and \(x[j-1] = x[j]\), for \(j >
0\), so the number of letter comparisons for~\(p\) is \(m-1\). An
example of pattern is \(p=a^{m}\).

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Morris-Pratt algorithm}

  To find the worst configuration of a pattern~\(p\) of length~\(m\)
  that yields the maximum number of letter comparisons, let us assume
  that the inner loop in the definition of \proc{Next} is executed as
  many times as possible, that is to say, the prefix test \(x[j-1] =
  x[j]\) fails until \(j = -1\).

  \bigskip

  In other words, \(\MPfailure{x}{}{j}\) must decrease as slowly as
  possible, which is actually the best case: \(\MPfailure{x}{}{j} = j
  - 1\).

  \bigskip

  In other words, the worst case is the best case followed by a letter
  that is not in the best case prefix: \(p = a^{m-1}b\), where \(a
  \neq b\). We know that the number of comparisons (equalities) for \(a^{m-1}\) is \(m-2\).

  \bigskip

  We need to add the comparisons (inequalities) of~\(b\) to all
  the~\(a\): \(x[j] \neq x[m-1]\) for \(0 \leqslant j < m - 1\), that
  is, \(m - 1\).

  \bigskip

  In total, the number of comparisons in the worst case is
  \((m-2)+(m-1) = 2m-3\).

\end{frame}

Let us parameterise the search with the failure function:
\begin{codebox}
\(\proc{Find} (p, t, \id{next})\)\\
\li \((i, j) \gets (0, 0)\)
\li \While \(i < \len{p}\) \LogAnd \(j < \len{t}\) \kw{do}
\li \> \If \(i = -1\) \LogOr \(p[i] = t[j]\)
\li    \Then \((i, j) \gets (i + 1, j + 1)\)
\li    \Else \(i \gets \id{next}[i]\)\RComment \(j\) invariant
       \End
\li \If \(i = \len{p}\)
\li \Then \(\proc{Find} \gets j - \len{p}\)\RComment Index of first occurrence
\li \Else \(\proc{Find} \gets -1\)\RComment Not found
\End
\end{codebox}

So the Morris-Pratt algorithm is

\(\proc{MP} (p, t)\)\\
